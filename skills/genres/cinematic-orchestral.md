# Cinematic and orchestral music

Film scores, orchestral pieces, musical-theatre numbers without a steady kit. Read this
with `skills/design-fundamentals/SKILL.md` whenever the drums aren't driving the song.

## Why these shows fail

Orchestral recordings are mastered loud and fairly even. A show that follows the energy
curve, or puts a small flash on every onset, comes out as **one texture for the whole
song**: an even sprinkle of ticks, nothing building, nothing landing. It can still score
well on the critique's correlation, because a flat curve is easy to follow. The first
Test Drive draft did exactly this: a constant DRL glow, tiny flashes everywhere, and the
leap and the climax barely different from the bars around them.

## How to choreograph it

1. **Phrases, not onsets.** The unit is the musical phrase (often 4-8 bars, or a melodic
   line from breath to breath). Give each phrase one gesture: a sweep, a swell, a
   call-and-response, a build. Use onsets only for real accents (brass stabs, timpani, a
   cymbal crash), never as a sprinkle.

2. **Set pieces.** Before writing code, name 4-7 set pieces, one per section, each with a
   motif no other section uses. For example: "the ostinato" (tight front/rear ping-pong),
   "the choir rise" (everything off, then a slow 8-bar swell to full), "flight" (long sweeps
   from front left around the car to the rear), "the leap" (black, then everything at once),
   "the grand theme" (main beams held, screen at full color, accents rolling). If two
   sections look alike in the timeline, one of them is wrong.

3. **Exaggerate the dynamics.** The recording is compressed; the show must not be. Quiet
   passages should be nearly dark (the screen breathing, one slow element). Peaks should
   be the whole car. Aim for at least a 3x difference in light energy between the
   quietest and loudest sections.

4. **Fades carry the soaring.** Swells in the strings and choir map to slow ramps: DRL and
   inner beams on 2 s ramps, the screen and accents fading in color and brightness over
   whole phrases. Chain them around the car (front left, front right, rear, cabin) so a
   swell travels.

5. **Hits must be the brightest, cleanest moments of the show.** Precede every big hit
   with darkness (half a bar to a bar), then fire the main beams, brake, reverse, screen
   white and every accent at once, with a closure move landing on it if one is available.
   Then decay: inner beams and DRL ramp off, the screen falls back to its color.

6. **No background glow.** Don't leave the DRL (or anything) on as a bed for long stretches.
   A light that is always on is invisible; it also flattens every hit. If a section needs a
   bed, use the screen at low brightness and keep the exterior dark between gestures.

7. **Use the main beams.** The outer and inner beams are the brightest lights on the car.
   In cinematic music they belong to the theme: hold them through the grand statement, swell
   the inner beams with the melody's climaxes, flash the outer beams on brass hits.

8. **Tempo is not the grid.** Orchestral tempo drifts and the analyzer's beat grid is often
   wrong. Time gestures to phrase starts and hits you find in the stems (the "other" stem
   holds strings and brass, "drums" holds percussion), and check them by ear against the
   overview plot.

## Check before you finish

- In the critique, no two neighboring sections should be flagged as looking alike.
- No exterior light should be on more than about 35% of the time.
- The biggest moments (the hits you named) should be the top 3 brightest moments of the show.
